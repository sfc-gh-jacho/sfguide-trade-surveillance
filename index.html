Overview

Needles in a Haystack of Orders

A surveillance analyst at a fictional broker-dealer reviews a trading day of about 200,000 orders. A dozen of them form manipulative patterns. Here you build the rules that find them, entirely in SQL, so every alert can be traced back to the raw orders that caused it.

A

Wash trading

Linked accounts trade with each other: volume without any real change in ownership.

B

Spoofing

Large orders placed and quickly cancelled to move the price, then a real trade on the other side.

C

Front-running

A trader's own account trades just ahead of a large client order they are handling.

D

Marking the close

One trader dominates volume in the final minutes to influence the closing price.

Prerequisites

Before You Begin

  • Any Snowflake edition for the detection steps. The optional reviewer masking step (12) needs Enterprise Edition.
  • ACCOUNTADMIN, or <YOUR_ROLE> with CREATE DATABASE, CREATE WAREHOUSE and CREATE ROLE on the account.
  • One Snowsight worksheet, run top to bottom. The script creates its own SURV_DEMO_DB database and SURV_WH warehouse and changes nothing else.
  • A few minutes of XSMALL compute, a fraction of one credit for a full run.

Step 1

Setup

Create a dedicated database and an XSMALL warehouse that suspends after 60 seconds idle. Everything else in the guide lives inside these two objects, so cleanup is a single drop.

Step 2

Traders, Accounts and Instruments

This step sets up 40 traders, each with a proprietary (PROP) account, 84 client accounts and 25 fictional tickers. Four client accounts are quietly linked in pairs by a shared beneficial owner. That hidden relationship is exactly what wash-trade detection relies on.

Step 3

One Day of Orders

This step generates 200,000 orders between the open and the close, deterministically so every run is identical. The normal flow is designed to be clean: 100–2,000 shares per order, about 15% cancelled, and never cancelled within 30 seconds. Any alert you see later therefore comes from a planted case, not from noise.

Step 5 · Rule A

Wash Trading

The rule joins accounts that share a beneficial owner, then looks for a buy and a sell in the same ticker of similar size and price within 60 seconds. On the screen they look like two unrelated clients. In reality one owner is trading with themselves to create fake volume.

Step 6 · Rule B

Spoofing with MATCH_RECOGNIZE

Spoofing is a sequence: three or more large orders on one side, each cancelled within 10 seconds, followed within 30 seconds by a fill on the opposite side. MATCH_RECOGNIZE describes that sequence as a pattern, like a regular expression over rows, and finds every match per account and ticker.

In DEFINE, use LAG() to look at the previous row. PREV() isn't available there, and conditions can't reference other pattern variables. That's why the pattern lists separate BUY and SELL variants.

Step 7 · Rule C

Front-running

For every large client order (20,000+ shares), the rule looks back two minutes for fills in the same trader's PROP account, in the same ticker and direction. Each alert says exactly how much was traded and how many seconds ahead of the client.

Step 8 · Rule D

Marking the Close

The rule aggregates executions in the final five minutes by trader and ticker, computes each trader's share of that ticker's volume with a window function, and uses QUALIFY to keep only traders above 20,000 shares and 30% of volume. That's two lines of filtering on top of one GROUP BY.

Step 11

Keep Alerts Fresh with a Dynamic Table

A Dynamic Table materializes the alerts and refreshes itself within its target lag, here one minute, whenever new orders land. No task or scheduler is needed. The script then inserts a new wash trade, and the alert appears after the next refresh.

Dynamic Tables don't support MATCH_RECOGNIZE. The live table covers the wash, front-running and marking-the-close rules, while spoofing runs on demand in the SURV_ALERTS view (or on a scheduled task). In this run both refreshes were FULL, not incremental, because of the joins and window functions in the rules. The step suspends the table at the end. Keep it suspended when you're not demoing.

Pseudonymized IDs for Reviewers

Wrap-up

Key Takeaways

You built four market-abuse rules, an explainable alert queue and an auto-refreshing alerts table, without moving data out of Snowflake.

Sequences in SQL

MATCH_RECOGNIZE expresses patterns like spoofing as a regular expression over rows, with no procedural code.

Explainable by design

Every alert carries a plain-English reason and links back to the exact orders that triggered it.

Fresh without pipelines

A Dynamic Table keeps alerts current within a target lag, with no orchestration to build or maintain.

Governed review

Masking gives second-line reviewers pseudonyms instead of identities, enforced on the data itself.
